<div class="section-label">Overview</div>

## What happened (and why it matters)

Canvas is one of the most widely used learning management systems (LMS) in higher education. When an LMS is disrupted, classes slow down. When an LMS ecosystem is attacked, it becomes a **privacy and trust problem**, not just a temporary outage.

Online and hybrid programs depend on digital workflows for coursework, communication, and student records. That means the stakes are higher: more accounts, more integrations, more data movement, and more opportunity for attackers to exploit weak links.

<div class="section-label">Canvas</div>

## Why Canvas is high-impact infrastructure

Canvas often becomes the “front door” to a student’s academic life:

- assignments and submissions
- instructor announcements and messages
- quizzes and exams
- gradebooks and feedback
- files and links to third‑party tools

Because so much academic activity depends on Canvas, any incident can have broad impact—especially during finals, when access to materials and deadlines matters most.

<div class="section-label">Risk</div>

## Why online + hybrid growth increases risk

Online education programs and hybrid college programs usually mean:

- more logins and more accounts
- more integrations (proctoring tools, SSO, video, e‑textbooks)
- more file uploads and messaging
- more admin workflows handled through vendor dashboards and reports

That’s great for flexibility—but it increases the number of pathways attackers can use: phishing, password reuse, misconfigured permissions, and vendor supply‑chain incidents.

<div class="section-label">Student privacy</div>

## What student data is at stake

Education systems often contain data students assume is private. Depending on the institution and connected tools, systems may store or connect to:

- names, emails, phone numbers
- student ID numbers
- enrollment records and course history
- grades and academic standing
- transcripts and credential verification flows

Even when a specific incident does <em>not</em> include certain sensitive fields, leaked identifiers and messages can still enable **targeted phishing and social engineering**.

For U.S. institutions, privacy obligations often involve education records under FERPA:
<a class="link-external" href="https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/Ferpa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">U.S. Department of Education: FERPA overview</a>.

<div class="section-label">Actions</div>

## How schools can respond (checklist)

Cybersecurity works best when it’s built into day‑to‑day operations. A practical baseline for online/hybrid programs includes:

### Require MFA (and make it easy)

Start with staff/admin accounts and expand coverage. NIST’s digital identity guidance is a useful baseline reference:
<a class="link-external" href="https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NIST SP 800‑63B</a>.

### Limit access to sensitive records

Use least‑privilege permissions for LMS admin roles, SIS integrations, exports/reporting tools, and third‑party app access.

### Patch quickly and monitor continuously

Keep software updated, track known exploited vulnerabilities, and monitor security logs—especially for unusual export/report patterns and token creation.

### Treat vendors as part of your security perimeter

Contracts and vendor governance should require logging/auditability, incident notification timelines, MFA support, and data minimization/retention limits.

### Train staff and exercise response plans

Phishing and credential theft remain common entry points. Regular training plus incident response exercises can reduce impact.

For a public‑sector oriented starting point, CISA’s education resources are helpful:
<a class="link-external" href="https://www.cisa.gov/audiences/educational-institutions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CISA: cybersecurity guidance for educational institutions</a>.

<div class="section-label">Students</div>

## What students can do

Students can reduce personal risk even when they don’t control vendor security:

- use unique passwords (a password manager helps)
- enable MFA where available
- avoid clicking “urgent account” links from random emails—navigate via your official school portal
- report suspicious messages to your school IT help desk
